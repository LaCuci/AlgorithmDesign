\section{Complexity Classes and Theory of Intractability (NP-Completeness)}

The objective is to categorize computational problems according to the complexity of the algorithms for their solution.

\subsection{Computational Problem}
\[
\Pi\subseteq\mathcal I\times\mathcal S\quad\text{(relation)},\qquad
(i,s)\in\Pi\quad[i\mathrel\Pi s]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s\text{ is a solution to }i.
\]

The computational problem is a set of instance solution pairs ($i,s$) such that $s$ is a solution to $i$.

It is a many to many correspondence: an instance $i\in\mathcal I$ may have many solutions $s\in\mathcal S$ and a solution $s\in\mathcal S$ may be a solution to many instances $i\in\mathcal I$.

Algorithm that solves $\Pi$ implements this function:
\[
A_\Pi:\mathcal I\longrightarrow\mathcal S,\qquad
\forall i\in\mathcal I:\quad i\mathrel\Pi A_\Pi(i)\in\mathcal S.
\]

\subsection{Complexity of a problem}

We are interested in upper/lower complexity bounds on algorithms for $\Pi$:
\[
\underbrace{\exists A_\Pi\text{ solving }\Pi:\ T_A(n)=O(f(n))}_{\text{UPPER BOUND}},
\]
\[
\underbrace{\forall A_\Pi\text{ solving }\Pi:\ T_A(n)=\Omega(f(n))}_{\text{LOWER BOUND}}.
\]

The lower bound is generally more difficult to prove than the upper bound.
The order of $f(n)$ captures the difficulty of $\Pi$.
